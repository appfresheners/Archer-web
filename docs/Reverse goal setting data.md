# Reverse Goal Setting — Justin Sung Framework Reference

> Synthesized from three Justin Sung video transcripts on reverse goal setting.
> This document is the canonical reference for how the system must behave.

---

## Why Normal Goal Setting Fails

Normal goal setting has a fundamental flaw: it focuses on **what** to achieve but not **how** to get there.

The typical pattern:

1. Set a goal (the trophy, the job, the exam result)
2. Attach a vague plan ("study harder", "try to get the job")
3. Hope effort alone gets you there

Problems with this:

- The path is invisible — you don't know which actions actually move you toward the goal
- Many paths exist, and some don't even look like they're going in the right direction
- Without visible progress, motivation collapses
- Luck plays an outsized role — some people work harder and still fail
- Setting the goal gives you a feeling of progress without creating actual progress

> "If the purpose of setting a goal is to help us achieve it, but setting the goal doesn't really make it easier or more predictable or more motivating to actually achieve it, then what's the point?" — Justin Sung

The outcome is also a symptom problem: **you can never directly control an outcome**. You can only control the processes, skills, habits, and attributes that raise the probability of achieving it.

---

## What Reverse Goal Setting Is

Reverse goal setting starts at the goal and works **backwards** to identify:

1. The type of person who would find achieving this goal easy
2. The skills, attributes, habits, and opportunities that person has
3. The gap between that person and who you are right now
4. The forces helping and blocking you from closing that gap
5. A focused, iterative action plan to close the gap

The insight is that **outcomes are symptoms of the person you are**. Rather than obsessing over the outcome, you invest in becoming the person for whom the outcome is a natural consequence.

> "A better wish would be: give me the skills, attributes, and habits that will let me make a million dollars." — Justin Sung

## The Five Steps

### Step 1 — Set Your Goal (and anchor on the Why)

Identify what you want to achieve. This can be a long-term vision (5–10 years) broken into a medium-term goal (ideally no more than 3 years, ideally 6–18 months).

**Critical principle:** The goal itself is likely to change. What should not change is the **underlying outcome** — the feeling, the situation, the position in life you are trying to reach. Always anchor on the _why_ behind the goal, not the goal itself.

- When you set a goal, what you really want is the way achieving it will make you feel
- If new information shows the goal won't deliver that feeling, **change the goal** — not the outcome
- Holding a goal rigidly when it no longer serves your true outcome is a trap

**Activity:**

- Write your long-term vision or direction (5–10 years, keep it broad)
- Write _why_ — what feeling or situation does this achieve for you?
- Give yourself explicit permission to pivot if the goal stops serving the why
- Break it into a medium-term goal (max 3 years; shorter is better)
- Specify the concrete requirements of that goal — what must actually be true for it to count as achieved?
  - If you can't specify the requirements, that gap is your immediate next step: go learn what they are

**On goal timeframes:**

- Goals further than 3 years out carry very high planning risk — one broken assumption cascades through everything
- Short sprints with constant re-evaluation outperform long rigid plans
- Don't use goals shorter than ~6 months for this process — the timeline is too short for meaningful skill development

---

### Step 2 — Define Your Future Self (Assess the Goal Level)

Imagine the **hypothetical person** who would find achieving this goal easy — almost inevitable. Not a superhuman, just the person who is clearly well-suited to it.

Ask: _What skills, attributes, habits, and opportunities does this person have that makes success nearly certain for them?_

**Rate each attribute out of 10** (this is a subjective scale — that's fine):

- You don't need 10/10 on everything
- The rating reflects how important that attribute is AND what level is required
- Be specific: don't just write "time management" — write what good time management actually looks like for this goal

**Categories to assess** (drawn from employability capitals research):

- Time management
- Task management and prioritization
- Focus and concentration
- Learning ability and efficiency
- Stress management and resilience
- Emotional regulation
- Organisation and planning
- Procrastination resistance
- Self-awareness and reflection
- Business knowledge / domain-specific knowledge
- Network and relationships
- Qualifications and credentials
- Access to opportunities and experiences

For each item:

- Give a target rating (1–10)
- Write a short comment explaining what that level looks like for this goal

This step turns anxiety and vague self-doubt into **explicit, named meta-goals**: the goals you must achieve in order to achieve the ultimate goal.

> "These are our meta goals. In other words, these are the goals that need to be achieved in order to achieve the ultimate goal." — Justin Sung

---

### Step 3 — Define Your Current Self (Assess Your Current Level)

Use the **exact same list of attributes** from Step 2.

For each attribute:

- Rate yourself currently out of 10
- Write a short comment explaining your honest self-assessment
- Calculate the **Gap** = Target rating − Current rating

**This gap is your personalized progression plan.** It tells you:

- Where you need to grow
- Which areas are your biggest blockers
- Where your effort will have the most impact on probability of success

**If you don't know how to rate yourself:** that's critical information. It means you lack self-awareness in that area, and developing it becomes your immediate next step — not the skill itself, but the ability to observe and assess it in yourself.

**Prioritization rule:**

- Don't try to close all gaps simultaneously — the brain cannot rewire that many things at once
- Find the **2–3 attributes** where:
  - The required level is highest, AND
  - Your current gap is widest
- Focus exclusively on those first
- After 1–2 months, reassess and shift focus to the next 2–3

> "It's better to have two or three things to focus on and then shift your focus to another two or three things one or two months later rather than having five or six things all trying to improve at the same time." — Justin Sung

**Benefits of this approach even if the goal is never reached:**

- You develop skills that transfer to any future goal
- You build genuine confidence (you know you can handle challenges regardless of their form)
- If the goal changes, the attributes you've built are still relevant

### Step 4 — Force Field Analysis

A visual technique for mapping what helps and blocks you from closing the gaps.

**Draw it like this:**

- You (current self) on the left
- Goal (future self / meta goal) on the right
- A curved barrier line in the middle — the "force field"

**Right side of the line — Barriers:**
What prevents you from developing the skills and attributes you need? Be specific and holistic — barriers don't have to be directly related to the goal. Anything that prevents you from doing the work counts.

Examples:

- Not enough time due to family commitments
- No system to track progress
- Unclear on what "good" looks like
- Unhealthy home environment
- Procrastination habits

**Left side of the line — Drivers and Resources:**

_Drivers_ — internal strengths and existing attributes that help you push through:

- Existing skills and habits
- Personal motivation and values
- Past relevant experience

_Resources_ — external assets you can deploy:

- Time available
- Money available
- People in your network
- Tools, courses, books, technology
- Access to environments, opportunities

**How to use it:**

- Look at each barrier
- Ask: which of my drivers or resources can I use to overcome or reduce this barrier?
- Some barriers can be eliminated or reduced — make a conscious decision about whether to do that
- Some barriers can't be removed — work around them

This step increases **agency**: instead of feeling blocked by vague obstacles, you have a map of the forces at play and a battle plan.

**Review cadence:** Every 1–2 weeks.

---

### Step 5 — Build the Action Plan

Distill everything from Steps 1–4 into **at most 2 focus points** for the immediate period.

Each focus point answers:

- **What** — which gap or barrier to address
- **When** — start now unless there's a sequencing reason not to
- **How** — if you don't know how, that becomes the first action: go find out

**Important:** The 2 focus points might not be about skill development. If a life barrier is blocking everything else, removing that barrier becomes the priority — even if it feels unrelated to the goal.

**Review cadences:**
| What to review | How often |
|---|---|
| Action plan (the 2 focus points) | Every 1–2 days |
| Force field analysis | Every 1–2 weeks |
| Current level ratings (gap assessment) | Every 2–4 weeks |
| Goal itself (still aligned with the why?) | Every 1–2 months |

**Timeline realism:**

- As a rule of thumb, you can improve any attribute by roughly 1 point per month with diligent effort
- Improvement slows as you approach the top (7→8 takes longer than 3→4)
- Getting to 10/10 on anything takes years, not months
- If the timeline to close your gaps is longer than the time you have before the goal, either extend the timeline or reconsider the goal — this is hard but necessary honesty

## Key Principles (Distilled)

| Principle                  | What it means                                                                          |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Outcomes are symptoms      | You can't control results. You can control the person you're becoming.                 |
| Anchor on the why          | Goals change. The underlying outcome you want should be your fixed point.              |
| Meta goals first           | Developing the required skills IS the goal. The trophy follows.                        |
| Focus beats breadth        | 2–3 attributes at a time, sequenced, not all at once.                                  |
| You win either way         | Even if the goal is missed, you've built transferable skills and raised your baseline. |
| Observation over intention | Time passing changes nothing. What you do with that time is everything.                |
| Realistic timelines        | Skill development is slow. Be conservative. Build in room for life.                    |
